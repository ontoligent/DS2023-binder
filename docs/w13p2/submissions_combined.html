Cross-Chart Themes

Statistical clarity did not equal rhetorical power

Every chart in the assignment is more statistically transparent than the polar plot in at least one dimension. The heatmap gives exact numeric values alongside color; the grouped bar makes within-month cause comparisons direct; the line chart makes slopes and velocities readable; the stacked area unifies the full 24-month arc. Students across all eight groups recognized and named these advantages clearly. Yet every group also concluded, independently and by the same logic, that their chart would have failed with Nightingale's actual audience. The assignment surfaces a gap that students rarely encounter in a data-analysis context: a chart can be maximally legible while being minimally persuasive. What makes a chart easy to read and what makes it capable of changing minds are different properties, and for Nightingale's audience — Victorian military officials and parliamentarians who actively discounted statistical arguments — those properties pulled in opposite directions.

The familiar-format problem

The single most consistent finding across all eight chart types was that every chart in the set was a variation on a format the audience already knew how to dismiss. Victorian officials had spent years receiving War Office reports in the form of bar charts, tables, and line graphs, and had developed a practiced indifference to them. Students analyzing charts as different as the heatmap (essentially a colored table), the stacked bar, the line chart, and the aggregate bar all converged on the same diagnosis: none of these would have stood out visually from what the audience had already filed and forgotten. The polar plot succeeded partly because it had no existing dismissal pathway — the audience had never seen anything shaped like it, and had to engage with it before they could reject it. Several students articulated this asymmetry directly: Genova (B) wrote it "looks like every other report they had already dismissed"; New (B) argued the polar plot "prompted curiosity before dismissal" in a way a stacked bar never could; and Narne (A) quoted the course framing that War Office tables were "read, filed, and ignored."

Annotation dependency and the standalone test

Nearly every group dealing with a temporal chart (A through F) identified a version of the same structural problem: the chart can display the pattern that reform worked, but it cannot explain that pattern without supplemental prose or a presenter. The before-and-after contrast is visible in the stacked bar, the line chart, and the stacked area — but attributing the decline to the sanitary commission requires a caption, an annotation, or a speaker. Students repeatedly asked the prompt's question — "could it do its work without a statistician standing next to it?" — and repeatedly answered no. Walton (B) put it most precisely: "the viewer sees the bars going down but has no reason from the chart alone to attribute that change to sanitation reform." Castellon (F) drew the sharpest contrast: "The rose carried its argument in the shape itself; the stacked area chart needs words to do the same work." This distinction between charts that contain their own argument and charts that merely display data for an argument to be constructed around them is the central analytic insight the assignment was designed to produce.

Temporal aggregation as a distinct failure mode

Charts G (Pie) and H (Bar aggregate) introduced a failure mode qualitatively different from the other six: they collapse time entirely, converting a crisis arc into a static proportion. Students analyzing the temporal charts (A–F) argued their charts failed to explain the before-and-after story; students analyzing G and H argued their charts failed to contain a before-and-after story at all. Gabby Kang (G) captured this most sharply: "It is an average over the full period, and averages do not create urgency." Chapuis (H) inventoried the specific absences: "no January 1855 spike, no sanitary commission line, no narrative arc." Morgan (G) went further, arguing the pie chart doesn't just omit time — it actively misrepresents the nature of the crisis, making it look like "a steady-state reality rather than a crisis with a beginning, a peak, and a possible resolution." The aggregate charts can prove that disease was the leading cause of death; they cannot demonstrate that the situation was acute, that it peaked, or that intervention resolved it.

Three-act placement clustered by chart type

When students placed their chart in Nightingale's three-act argument (problem → cause → solution), their answers clustered predictably by chart structure. The aggregate charts — G (Pie) and H (Bar) — were placed in the cause phase unanimously, since showing disease as the dominant cause is the only argument they can make. The stacked area chart (F) was placed in the solution phase unanimously, because its most compelling visual feature is the total-height collapse after the sanitary commission. Most of the other temporal charts (A Heatmap, C Grouped Bar, D Line for many students) landed in the cause phase, though the stacked bar (B) and line chart (D) divided students between cause and solution depending on which visual feature they weighted. No chart was consistently assigned to the problem phase, and no student argued any chart could carry its assigned position without supplemental framing. The polar plot's rhetorical advantage — implicit in every response without being named — is that it functions in all three acts simultaneously: the two-ring structure poses the problem, the dominant blue wedges name the cause, and the collapse of those wedges in the second ring demonstrates the solution.

Thematic Summary

A. Heatmap

Color Encoding Beats Polar Area Comparison

Nearly every student identified the heatmap's color gradient as a more direct encoding than the polar plot's wedge areas for cross-cause, cross-month comparison. Chen noted that "dark red squares in the January–March 1855 period immediately stand out" in a way the circular layout obscures. Damle specified that viewers could "directly compare cell values across rows without having to mentally calculate the size of different wedges or navigate a rotating axis." Garrett, Narne, and Tang also pointed to the dual encoding of exact numeric values plus color as a particular advantage over the polar plot's purely geometric encoding.

Heatmap Reads as Another Ignored Table

The most widely shared argument for the chart's rhetorical failure was that it would have blended in with the War Office reports Victorian officials already dismissed. Tang put it most sharply: "The heatmap is essentially a colored table, and while the color makes it slightly more readable, it does not break from the visual language of the reports they had already dismissed." Press agreed that the audience "had already filed away countless War Office reports" and would have read it as "more of the same." Damle, Ye, Kim, and Narne made nearly identical observations.

Abstract Color Scale Needs Expert Mediation

Multiple students argued the heatmap cannot do its rhetorical work without a statistician present to explain the color-to-magnitude mapping. Chen framed this as an "abstract task for people without statistical training," while Hawkins imagined a mediator needing to say "the dark red cells mean soldiers are dying at catastrophic rates." Mathuria and Ye both noted the encoding requires a viewer to understand that "darker means higher" — an assumption that could not be taken for granted with Nightingale's audience. Kim added that the heatmap format itself was likely unfamiliar to Victorians, compounding the need for guided interpretation.

Chart Belongs in Cause, Not Solution

Every student placed the heatmap in the cause act of Nightingale's argument, with the disease row's dominance across nearly all months as the specific evidence. All twelve also argued it cannot carry that position alone because it lacks the before-and-after reform structure needed for the solution act. Chen said it "doesn't directly show the sharp decline after sanitation reforms"; Tang noted that without annotation "a viewer would not connect that lightening to a specific intervention"; and Press observed a viewer might conclude disease deaths are high "but would not necessarily arrive at the conclusion that the sanitary intervention worked."

Data Displays Without Performing an Argument

Several students distinguished between presenting data and making a rhetorical argument, finding the heatmap capable only of the former. Press wrote that "it presents data but does not perform an argument," while Tang argued Nightingale "needed a chart that could walk into the room and argue by itself." Narne described it as lacking "narrative momentum," and Yeddanapalli drew a pointed contrast: the rose chart "shows" the difference through shape while the heatmap merely "tells" it, implying a passivity that undermines persuasion. Bajaj and Garrett both noted the absence of emotional pull as the core deficiency.

B. Stacked Bar

Linear Encoding Clarifies Temporal Trends and Totals

Nearly all students identified the stacked bar's linear x-axis and uniform bar heights as a major advantage over the polar plot for reading month-by-month change. Chien noted that tracking total bar height over time makes the January 1855 peak and subsequent decline immediately apparent, in a way the polar plot's overlapping wedges obscure. Walton added that the consistent y-axis scale allows precise quantitative comparison across the full 24-month period. Hermes specifically praised the uniform rectangular width for making the disproportion between disease and combat deaths more legible than the radially-arranged wedges of the polar plot.

Familiar Format Killed Persuasive Power with Victorian Audience

Every student argued that the chart's most fatal flaw was its visual familiarity: Victorian generals, parliamentarians, and Queen Victoria had already been handed and ignored War Office reports in this exact format. Genova put it most bluntly — "it looks like every other report they had already dismissed." New argued that the polar plot worked precisely because "nobody had seen anything like it," prompting curiosity before dismissal, while a stacked bar generates no such curiosity. Lam and Yeddanapalli both emphasized that the chart was not memorable enough to be discussed after the meeting.

Chart Cannot Close the Argument Without Annotations

A strong consensus emerged that the stacked bar shows a decline after March 1855 but cannot explain what caused it without external framing. Walton stated the problem precisely: "the viewer sees the bars going down but has no reason from the chart alone to attribute that change to sanitation reform." Lam called the unexplained decline "the gap where it fails as a standalone persuasive tool." Chien and Yeddanapalli both noted that only the annotated version — with dates marking Nightingale's arrival and the Sanitary Commission — could carry the solution argument.

Split on Three-Act Placement: Cause vs. Solution

Students divided almost evenly on whether the chart belongs in the cause or solution phase. McIntyre, Hermes, New, and Pribram placed it in the cause phase, arguing that the consistent dominance of the blue disease segment makes the root problem legible. Chien, Genova, Lam, Walton, and Yeddanapalli placed it in the solution phase, pointing to the sharp post-commission decline as its strongest visual feature. Barrow was the outlier, arguing it could fit all three phases but held the least weight in the solution because the connection between the sanitary commission and declining disease deaths is not visually explicit.

Chart Presents Data but Does Not Argue a Case

Several students distinguished between a chart that informs and one that persuades, placing the stacked bar firmly in the former category. Hermes argued the chart "doesn't present itself as a call to action but rather as a summary of information," exactly the failure mode of prior War Office reports. Pribram drew the same line: it "presents data for a viewer to interpret, rather than delivering an argument that hits them before they know what it is." McIntyre put it in terms of Nightingale's rhetorical situation: without someone narrating the chart, "the visual pattern does no persuasive work on its own."

C. Grouped Bar

Side-by-Side Bars Clarify Within-Month Cause Comparisons

Every student identified the grouped bar chart's core advantage as the ability to compare causes of death directly within a single month. Bontagarla, Sam, and Miles each noted that the three bars per cluster make the disproportion between disease and injury deaths immediately legible, eliminating the mental effort required to separate overlapping wedge areas in the polar chart. Wang added that this encoding permits "precise month-by-month cause comparisons at a glance," and nearly all students anchored their argument with the same concrete example: the January 1855 disease bar towering over the injury and other bars in the same cluster.

Familiar Chart Format Killed Persuasive Novelty

All nine students argued that the grouped bar chart would have failed Nightingale's audience because it looked too much like the conventional War Office charts that Victorian decision-makers had already received and dismissed. Georgievski framed this directly as "visual familiarity rather than novelty," while Miles wrote that the format "offers no visual novelty that would have made it stand out or stick in memory after the meeting." Yi put it most starkly, calling the chart "familiar, dense, and unemotional," and Panda argued it "communicates data without communicating urgency."

72-Bar Density Overwhelms Without a Presenter

Students consistently identified the sheer volume of bars — 72 across 24 months — as a compounding failure alongside unfamiliarity. Choi counted them explicitly and Wang noted there is "no inherent visual hierarchy guiding the eye toward the most important comparison." Bontagarla and Georgievski both argued the chart "cannot do its work without a statistician standing next to it," while Le warned that without a speaker to highlight the January 1855 spike, the key comparison "gets lost in the noise." Panda made the sharpest rhetorical point: the chart "requires a viewer to decide what to look at before it can make its argument" — exactly what a hostile audience will not do.

Chart Belongs in the Cause Phase, Not Alone

Every student placed the grouped bar chart in the cause act of Nightingale's three-part argument, agreeing it effectively demonstrates that disease deaths dominated injury and other deaths throughout the crisis. All nine also concluded it cannot carry that position unaided. Georgievski, Miles, Sam, and Wang each observed that the chart fails to make the solution — the post-sanitary-commission decline — visually apparent, meaning it proves the problem without pointing toward the remedy. Wang summarized the consensus: "the crowded layout and lack of built-in narrative direction mean the chart needs supplemental framing to point viewers toward the conclusion."

D. Line

Continuous Lines Clarify Temporal Trends and Slopes

Nearly every student identified the line chart's core analytic advantage as its ability to make time-based patterns readable through continuous encoding. Brown noted that continuous lines let viewers follow a path rather than compare discrete segments, while Minaya argued the chart made the velocity of change legible — the rise in late 1854 was steep, the post-commission decline even steeper. Jaldin added that slope changes make the direction and speed of shifts visible in a way the polar plot's circular layout actively resists. Duong and Wang both emphasized that the full 24-month span is easier to read as a linear progression in a line chart than across a circular axis.

Familiar Format Rendered It Dismissible to Nightingale's Audience

Every student argued the line chart would have failed rhetorically because it matched the chart type the War Office had already deployed without effect. Chu, Duong, and Minaya all stated explicitly that Victorian officials had been handed line charts and data graphs for years and had trained themselves to discount them. Wang put it most directly: the chart "matches exactly the type of conventional chart that the War Office had already deployed to no effect." Linda Le added that unlike the polar plot, the line chart lacked the visual unfamiliarity that made it hard to dismiss.

Chart Shows Data but Cannot Argue Without Annotation

Students consistently distinguished between the chart's ability to display patterns and its inability to convert those patterns into persuasion without external framing. Brown articulated this as the polar plot having the story embedded in the visual itself, while the line chart would need supplemental framing. Vivian Lee and Linda Le both wrote versions of the same formulation: the chart shows what happened but not why it matters, making it documentation rather than argument. Chu, Sardar, and Wang all argued that without annotation attributing the post-commission decline to a specific intervention, viewers would see the drop but not connect it to the case for reform.

Solution Phase Is the Chart's Natural Home

A large majority of students — including Chu, Jaldin, Le, Minaya, Sardar, Wang, and Yoon — placed the line chart most naturally in the solution phase, with its sharpest visual feature being the steep decline in disease deaths after the sanitary commission. Several students (Brown, Duong, Vivian Lee, Linda Le, Xinhao Wang) hedged by arguing it could also serve the cause phase, since the dominance of disease deaths across the full period is equally visible. The consistent caveat was that it cannot carry either phase alone: the viewer sees the before-and-after contrast but needs supplemental context to attribute the change to sanitation reform.

Legibility Without Emotional Force: a Rhetorical Mismatch

Students repeatedly drew a contrast between the line chart's technical legibility and its rhetorical inertness — it is readable but not compelling. Ethan Le wrote that it "tells viewers what happened but doesn't make them feel why it matters," and Vivian Lee said it "reads as a record rather than an argument." Brown noted that comparing overlapping lines is "not as visceral or immediate as a dramatic visual shape," and Jaldin argued that without visual novelty there is no hook to override an audience predisposed to dismissal. Diana Wang made the structural point most precisely: the chart requires a viewer already willing to engage with statistics — exactly what Nightingale's audience was not.

E. Area

Filled Areas Enable Intuitive Spatial Comparison

Nearly every student identified the filled area encoding as the chart's core advantage over the polar plot. Eichberg noted that the disease area in January 1855 "dwarfs" the other causes "in a way that is more spatially intuitive than comparing overlapping wedge areas," and Mohamed described that area as "visually massive." Shah added a temporal dimension, observing that the filled region shows not just magnitude but duration — disease dominance from July 1854 through March 1855. Gurindapalli specifically credited the unstacked format for enabling direct comparison of absolute values across causes for any given month.

Conventional Format Alienated a Statistics-Resistant Audience

All nine students argued the chart would have failed because it too closely resembled charts the Victorian audience had already dismissed. Kale wrote that the audience "had been trained to dismiss exactly this kind of conventional statistical chart," and Park observed it was "a variation on the line chart, which the War Office had already been using." Shah put it most precisely: the chart "requires active engagement — interpreting regions, drawing conclusions — which is exactly the kind of active engagement an audience predisposed to dismiss statistics will not provide." Cameron framed the contrast sharply: the polar chart is "striking and memorable whereas this one is not."

Chart Cannot Argue Without a Statistician Present

Students repeatedly argued that the area chart displays data but cannot make a case by itself. Eichberg wrote that "without a statistician standing beside it, the audience would not know what they were looking at or why it mattered," and Mohamed said someone needed to be present to "frame the scale of the disease area as evidence of preventable deaths." Gurindapalli noted the chart "does not offer a narrative hook; it displays the data without guiding the viewer toward any particular conclusion." Webster added that it "requires the viewer to interpret without built-in narrative direction."

Chart Fits Cause or Problem Phase but Cannot Stand Alone

Students were divided between placing the chart in the problem phase and the cause phase, but agreed it could not carry either position unaided. Cameron and Eichberg placed it in the problem phase because the spike makes the scale of the crisis viscerally apparent. Kale, Mohamed, Shah, and Webster placed it in the cause phase because it shows disease as the dominant cause relative to injury and other deaths. All groups concluded, as Park summarized, that it "documents what happened but does not guide the viewer toward the solution or make the case that reform was effective."

F. Stacked Area

Continuous Axis Unifies the Full Narrative

Every student identified the stacked area chart's single continuous x-axis as its primary advantage over Nightingale's polar plot. Castellon argued that the rose's two-ring structure "requires the viewer to mentally merge two plots," while the stacked chart makes "the rise and fall of total mortality into one unbroken visual narrative." Ha and Xiao made the same point in nearly identical terms, with Ha noting the chart "tells the whole story in one frame without requiring the viewer to mentally connect two separate rings." This consensus was striking in its uniformity — no student identified any other encoding advantage as primary.

Stacked Band Encoding Too Complex for Untrained Audiences

All eight students argued that the stacked area chart's encoding would have failed with Victorian generals and parliamentarians because reading non-baseline bands requires subtraction — a non-obvious interpretive operation. Kira Chung was precise about the mechanism: "only the bottom band's height is directly readable from the x-axis baseline, while the other bands must be read by their band widths." Michael Chung and Lin both framed this as an active burden on the audience — Lin writing that "for an audience already predisposed to dismiss statistical arguments, this additional interpretive burden would have been fatal." Sim offered the sharpest formulation: the chart is "visually complex without being visually dramatic."

Chart Lacks the Polar Plot's Pre-Rational Visual Impact

Several students argued that the stacked area chart's failure was not just cognitive but rhetorical — it could not arrest an unwilling audience before they chose to disengage. Castellon argued the rose "hits the audience before they know what they're seeing," while the stacked chart "presents data logically but does not hit the audience." Lin invoked "visual shock value" and Paruthipattu cited a lack of "visual memorability." Xiao noted it "does not stand out visually the way the polar plot's unusual shape does." This theme — that persuasion required a pre-rational visual hook — was distinct from the encoding-difficulty argument and shows students thinking about rhetoric, not just legibility.

Chart Belongs in Solution Phase but Cannot Stand Alone

Every student placed the stacked area chart in Nightingale's solution phase, citing the visible collapse in total chart height after the sanitary commission as its most persuasive visual feature. But all eight also concluded the chart cannot carry that position without annotation and prose. Castellon drew the contrast most sharply: "The rose carried its argument in the shape itself; the stacked area chart needs words to do the same work." Kira Chung and Sim both specified the gap — the audience would see the decline but could not connect it to the sanitary commission without external framing. Paruthipattu summarized the consensus: the chart "remains a data summary rather than a persuasive argument."

G. Pie (aggregate)

Pie Chart Excels at Aggregate Proportional Legibility

Every student identified the pie chart's core strength as making the proportional share of deaths by cause immediately legible through slice size. Chappell noted it allows readers to "quickly understand that the dominant cause of death is disease, without needing any further explanation," while Crater observed that this comparison "is harder to perform in the polar chart, where the monthly breakdown and circular layout make aggregate comparisons less intuitive." Southworth and Lohman both emphasized that the slice encoding communicates disease dominance "without ambiguity" and more cleanly than the polar plot's month-by-month area encoding.

Temporal Erasure Destroys Persuasive Urgency

All ten students identified the loss of temporal context as the chart's fatal flaw for Nightingale's purposes. Gabby Kang put it most sharply: "It is an average over the full period, and averages do not create urgency." Crater and Lohman both noted that the chart "reduces 24 months of data to a single proportion," eliminating the before-and-after structure needed to show that reform worked. Morgan argued this makes the crisis look like "a steady-state reality rather than a crisis with a beginning, a peak, and a possible resolution," which precisely inverts the argument Nightingale needed to make.

Familiar Chart Type Lacks Memorability and Novelty

Several students argued that the pie chart's very accessibility worked against it because familiarity breeds dismissal. Evans noted it "could not stand on its own as a memorable visual artifact, since pie charts were already common and familiar," while Lohman specified that it "would not have stood out from the conventional visualizations the audience had already dismissed." Mia Kang linked memorability directly to persuasive failure: pie charts were "not striking enough to persist in memory after a parliamentary meeting." Paul synthesized both weaknesses, writing that the chart "lacks both narrative and visual novelty."

Chart Belongs in Cause, Not Solution

Every student placed the pie chart in the cause section of the three-act argument, but every student also argued it cannot carry that position alone. Chappell framed this most directly: "It is a summary, not a story, and you cannot convince an audience to reform the Army with a summary." Southworth and Crater both concluded it would need "supplemental charts and prose" to function within the three-act structure. Gabby Kang allowed it could "support the cause argument as one of several pieces of evidence but cannot stand alone as the persuasive centerpiece," a formulation echoed in nearly identical terms by Paul, Morgan, and Hamilton.

H. Bar (aggregate)

Bar Height Beats Area Encoding for Direct Comparison

Every student identified bar height on a shared axis as more legible than the polar plot's area encoding across a circular layout. Chapuis framed it as avoiding "visually integrating wedge areas over 24 months," while Kester specified the concrete contrast — disease at ~350 per thousand versus far lower injury rates — that bar height makes immediately readable. Hawkes and Yan both emphasized that height is the most intuitive encoding for magnitude, requiring no geometric interpretation.

Aggregation Erases the Temporal Narrative Entirely

All nine students identified the loss of time as the chart's fatal flaw. Chapuis named the missing specifics most concretely — "no January 1855 spike, no sanitary commission line, no narrative arc" — while Najdzinowicz pointed to "the spike in early 1855 and the decline after reform" as the precise information that disappears. Stadler and Manikandan both stressed that without a before-and-after arc, the data produces no urgency and cannot move an audience to act.

Familiar Chart Type the Audience Had Already Dismissed

Multiple students argued that the bar chart's conventional format was itself a liability with Nightingale's audience. Crow and Kester noted that the War Office had already been producing similar charts that generals and Parliament had ignored. Najdzinowicz put it most directly: the chart "uses a familiar and conventional format that the audience had already been dismissing in War Office reports," and Peterson added that the form was neither memorable nor visually arresting.

Chart Belongs in Cause, Cannot Carry It Alone

Every student placed the bar chart in the cause section of the three-act argument, citing its clear ranking of disease as the dominant cause of death by mean rate. But all nine also concluded it cannot carry that position alone. Hawkes drew the sharpest distinction — the chart functions as "documentation" rather than "persuasion" without supplemental context — and Crow noted it cannot move the audience "from acknowledging the information to demanding change."

True Statement, but Not Urgent or Actionable

Several students distinguished between the chart being factually correct and being rhetorically effective. Manikandan articulated this most cleanly: "That statement is true but not urgent, and without urgency, it cannot move an audience to act." Stadler echoed the point with "averages do not create urgency," and Chapuis framed the audience's likely response as dismissal — "set aside as more of the same" — because average-based statistical reports were already a familiar genre they had learned to ignore.

Appendix: Glossary

Fundamental concepts employed across the student texts and analyses, in alphabetical order.

Aggregation
The process of collapsing multiple data points into a single summary statistic — for example, reducing 24 months of mortality data to a mean death rate per cause. Aggregation trades temporal or dimensional specificity for simplicity. Charts G (Pie) and H (Bar) aggregate across the full period, eliminating the before-and-after structure central to Nightingale's argument. Students analyzing these charts consistently identified aggregation as the reason they could prove disease was the leading cause of death but could not demonstrate that the crisis was acute or that reform resolved it.
Annotation
Text, labels, or reference lines added to a chart to supply context that the visual encoding alone cannot convey. In the annotated versions of charts B and C, vertical dotted lines mark Nightingale's arrival in November 1854 and the Sanitary Commission in March 1855. Students repeatedly identified annotation as a prerequisite for charts that could display a pattern but could not explain it — charts that pass the data-accuracy test but fail the standalone test.
Annualized death rate
The number of deaths per 1,000 soldiers per year, computed as M = (D / S) × 1,000 × 12, where D is deaths from a given cause in a given month and S is the army's average strength that month. The annualized rate allows fair comparison across months with different troop strengths. It is the quantity encoded in all eight alternative charts and in the polar plot itself. The formula produces values like 1,022.8 for disease deaths in January 1855 — a figure students across nearly all chart types cited as the clearest evidence of the crisis peak.
Area encoding
A visual encoding in which data values are represented by the area of a shape — a wedge, a filled region, or a bubble. Area encoding is less perceptually accurate than length/height encoding because viewers cannot reliably compare areas without counting or measuring. Nightingale's polar plot uses area encoding for the wedges; the unstacked and stacked area charts do as well. The heatmap and bar charts avoid it by encoding magnitude as color intensity and bar height respectively, which students consistently identified as an analytic advantage over the polar plot.
Cause phase
The second act in Nightingale's three-act argument: establishing that preventable disease — not combat wounds — was the dominant cause of soldier deaths. Most chart types in this study were placed in the cause phase by their assigned students, particularly those showing the ongoing dominance of disease deaths across all months (A, C, G, H). Students who placed their chart in the cause phase also uniformly concluded it could not carry that position alone without supplemental framing or a companion chart addressing the solution.
Data literacy
The capacity to read, interpret, and critically evaluate data visualizations and statistical arguments. Nightingale's target audience — Queen Victoria, Army generals, and Members of Parliament — had low data literacy and had developed an active skepticism toward statistical presentations. Students cited this repeatedly as the reason a statistically superior chart would still fail rhetorically: legibility assumes a motivated and capable reader, which Nightingale's audience was not.
Encoding
The mapping of a data variable to a visual property such as position, length, height, area, color, or angle. Different encodings make different comparisons easy or hard. Length/height encoding (bar charts) is more perceptually accurate than area encoding for comparing magnitudes. Color encoding (heatmaps) makes outliers salient but requires viewers to understand the scale. The choice of encoding determines what a chart makes immediately visible and what it hides — a central issue in every group's analysis of their assigned chart.
Familiar-format problem
The rhetorical failure that occurs when a chart uses a visual form the audience has already learned to dismiss. Nightingale's specific audience had spent years receiving War Office reports as bar charts, tables, and line graphs without acting on them. Every chart in this study was identified as a variation on a format the audience could recognize and set aside before engaging with its content. The polar plot avoided this problem by being visually unlike anything the audience had seen, forcing engagement before dismissal was possible.
Length / height encoding
A visual encoding in which data values are represented by the length or height of a bar or line segment measured from a common baseline. Length encoding is among the most perceptually accurate encodings available, allowing reliable comparison across values. Bar charts (B, C, H) and line charts (D) use length or height encoding, which students universally identified as making precise comparisons between causes or months easier than the polar plot's area encoding. The accuracy advantage, however, did not translate into rhetorical advantage with Nightingale's audience.
Narrative arc
A story structure with a beginning, development, and resolution. In Nightingale's campaign: the onset of catastrophic disease deaths (problem), the identification of disease as a preventable cause (cause), and the decline of deaths following sanitary reform (solution). Students repeatedly assessed whether their chart contained a narrative arc or merely displayed data. Charts that preserve time can show the arc, but most cannot convey it as a self-contained argument without annotation. The polar plot's two-ring structure embeds the arc visually: the right ring shows the crisis, the left ring shows the resolution, and the juxtaposition poses a question the data answers.
Polar plot (rose diagram)
Nightingale's distinctive chart from her 1858 report, in which 12 months of data are arranged as wedge-shaped sectors around a circle, with each wedge's area proportional to the annualized death rate for a given cause and month. Two plots are shown side by side — April 1854–March 1855 and April 1855–March 1856 — so the reader must ask what changed between them. The chart's unusual shape made it visually novel, its area-encoding corrected the error in Nightingale's earlier "batwing" diagram, and its structure embedded the three-act argument without requiring prose to explain it. It is the benchmark against which all eight alternative charts in this study are measured.
Problem phase
The first act in Nightingale's three-act argument: establishing that soldiers were dying at catastrophic rates. Few students in this study assigned their chart primarily to the problem phase; most assigned it to the cause or solution phase. Students who analyzed the unstacked area chart (E) were the most likely to place it in the problem phase, arguing that the sheer visual mass of the disease region during the crisis peak made the scale of the problem viscerally apparent even before identifying its cause.
Proportional ink
The design principle that the amount of ink (or visual area) used to represent a data value should be proportional to that value. Nightingale's first version of the polar plot — the "batwing" diagram — violated this principle by mapping death rates to the radius of wedges. Because the area of a circle sector increases as the square of the radius, this exaggerated differences between months. Her revised version corrected the error by mapping death rates to area directly, using the formula r = √(A / (θ/2)) to derive the appropriate radius from the target area.
Rhetorical effectiveness
The capacity of a communication to change the beliefs or actions of a specific audience. In this study, rhetorical effectiveness is consistently treated as distinct from statistical accuracy: a chart can be maximally accurate while being minimally persuasive. Students across all eight chart types named some version of this distinction — most sharply articulated by Press (A): "it presents data but does not perform an argument," and by Castellon (F): "the rose carried its argument in the shape itself."
Sanitary Commission
The body established by the British government in March 1855 to improve sanitation in military hospitals in Crimea, partly through Nightingale's advocacy. Its work — flushing sewers, improving ventilation, removing contaminated material — is associated with the dramatic decline in disease deaths visible in the data after that date. The commission represents the "solution" act in Nightingale's argument: evidence that reform works and that more reform is warranted. Students repeatedly noted that charts showing a post-March 1855 decline cannot name the commission as its cause without annotation.
Solution phase
The third act in Nightingale's three-act argument: demonstrating that sanitary reform measurably reduced disease deaths, proving that further reform would save more lives. The stacked area chart (F) was placed in this phase unanimously; the stacked bar (B) and line chart (D) were divided between cause and solution depending on which visual feature students weighted. No student argued any chart could carry the solution phase without annotation identifying the Sanitary Commission and connecting the visual pattern to the policy claim.
Standalone test
The criterion of whether a chart can make its argument without a statistician, presenter, or caption standing beside it. The polar plot passes this test because its unusual shape forces engagement, and its two-ring structure poses a question — what changed between the two years? — that the data itself answers. Students applied this test to every chart in the study and found all of them wanting in some respect: the temporal charts can display the before-and-after pattern but cannot explain it; the aggregate charts cannot display the pattern at all.
Temporal context
The presence of a time dimension in a data visualization, allowing viewers to see when events occurred, how conditions changed, and in what sequence. Charts A through F preserve temporal context — each month appears as a distinct data point. Charts G (Pie) and H (Bar aggregate) collapse it into a single summary. Students analyzing G and H consistently identified the loss of temporal context as the decisive reason those charts could not support Nightingale's argument: without time, there is no peak to establish urgency and no decline to demonstrate that reform worked.
Three-act argument
The rhetorical structure Nightingale used in her 1858 campaign: (1) Problem — soldiers are dying at catastrophic rates; (2) Cause — most deaths are from preventable disease, not combat; (3) Solution — sanitation reforms demonstrably reduced those deaths. The structure requires visual and prose elements working in concert. The polar plot is unusual in embedding all three acts simultaneously: the two rings show the problem scale (act 1), the dominant blue wedges identify the cause (act 2), and the collapse of those wedges in the second ring demonstrates the solution (act 3).
Urgency
The emotional and political pressure a visualization creates by making a problem feel immediate and actionable rather than abstract or historical. The polar plot generated urgency by making the scale of preventable deaths viscerally apparent and by juxtaposing the crisis year against the reform year in a single frame. Students analyzing the aggregate charts (G and H) identified the absence of urgency as a specific failure mode: Gabby Kang (G) wrote that "averages do not create urgency," and Stadler (H) echoed that phrase directly. Without urgency, a chart can inform without motivating.
Visual novelty
The quality of a chart being visually unfamiliar or surprising, which forces a viewer to engage with it before deciding whether to dismiss it. Visual novelty is not an intrinsic feature of a chart type but a property of the chart relative to its audience's prior experience. The polar plot had high visual novelty for Victorian parliamentarians who had never seen a rose diagram; a stacked bar had none, because the audience had already developed a dismissal reflex toward conventional chart types. Students across all eight chart groups cited the lack of visual novelty as a reason their chart would have failed with Nightingale's audience.
Zymotic diseases
The Victorian term for infectious or epidemic diseases — including cholera, typhus, and dysentery — that were considered "preventable" because they spread through contaminated water and poor sanitation rather than through combat. In Nightingale's data, the "Disease" column corresponds to zymotic diseases, and the blue or light-blue color in the charts represents their death rate. The term appears in the legend of the original polar plot: "blue wedges … represent area for area the deaths from Preventable or Mitigable Zymotic diseases." The preventability of these deaths is the moral core of Nightingale's argument: the army was not being killed by the enemy but by conditions the government could fix.
